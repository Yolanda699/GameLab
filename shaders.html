Shader Lab

Shaders I wrote,
running on your GPU.

Six fragment shaders, no libraries. Every canvas below compiles the exact source printed underneath it, so you can read the code, drag the parameters and edit it in place. It is the fastest way I know to show that I understand what a material graph is doing underneath.

Start with the fog Production work
Why this page exists. Technical art job posts ask for shader authoring, PBR fundamentals, profiling and 3D maths. Node graphs are how I ship, but writing the same effects by hand is how I learned what each node costs and why it behaves the way it does.
How to read it. Sliders are uniforms. Edit live turns the code block into a real editor – change a number, and the shader recompiles on the next keystroke. Compile errors show up exactly as the GPU reports them.
01

Layered mist & ridges

Volumetric fog · fBm
live GLSL

Five ridge silhouettes built from fractal noise, each with a band of fog pooling on top and firelight bouncing up from below. The cursor parallaxes the layers.

How it works

  • Value noise + fBm. A hash-based noise sampled over 5 octaves, each double the frequency and half the amplitude. This is the same building block as a Perlin/Simplex node in a material graph.
  • Ridges from 1D noise. Sampling the noise along x only gives a height per column; a smoothstep across that height turns it into an antialiased silhouette.
  • Depth cues. Nearer layers are darker, move faster and sit lower, so parallax alone reads as distance.

In The Feeding this is Exponential Height Fog with volumetric scattering plus a directional light. Writing it by hand is how I learned what the fog density and scattering parameters are actually doing. The Feeding →

Cook-Torrance PBR sphere

      GGX · Smith · Schlick
    

  

  

    

      

        
        
live GLSL

      

      

      
A full metallic-roughness BRDF written from scratch: GGX distribution, Smith visibility, Schlick Fresnel, plus a stand-in environment probe. Drag the sliders the way you would in a material instance.

    

    

      
How it works

      
  • D · G · F. GGX normal distribution for the highlight shape, Smith geometry for shadowing between microfacets, Schlick Fresnel for grazing angles. The same three terms Unreal and Unity evaluate per pixel.
  • Metallic workflow. F0 lerps from 4% for dielectrics to the base colour for metals, and diffuse is scaled to zero as metalness rises – exactly why a metal has no albedo.
  • Fake IBL. The environment function widens the sun and flattens the gradient with roughness, which is what a prefiltered mip chain does on a real reflection probe.
  • ACES tone map and a gamma curve at the end, because lighting must be done in linear space.

      
 This is the model behind every material I authored for The Feeding, and behind the cross-platform validation benchmarks I helped set up at Tencent: if albedo and roughness are sane here, they are sane in engine.

    03
    

      
Water surface

      Ray-plane · panning normals · Fresnel
    

  

  

    

      

        
        
live GLSL

      

      

      
A ray-plane intersection gives a receding water surface, two panning noise layers become a procedural normal map, and Fresnel decides where you see sky and where you see the water body.

    

    

      
How it works

      
  • Procedural normal map. The height field is sampled three times and differenced, which is how a normal map is generated from a height map – here it happens per pixel instead of being baked.
  • Two panning layers at different scales and directions, the standard trick for water that never visibly repeats.
  • Fresnel + absorption. Glancing angles reflect the sky; steep angles show a Beer-Lambert absorption colour. Those are the scattering and absorption coefficients of Unreal’s Single Layer Water.
  • Distance fade on the normal strength keeps the far field from aliasing – the hand-written version of a mip bias.

      
 M7 and M8 in The Feeding are two instances of one water master: same normals, different absorption and roughness for the day town and the night river.

    04
    

      
Soap bubble

      Fresnel rim · thin film · refraction
    

  

  

    

      

        
        
live GLSL

      

      

      
The bubble from Bubble Factory, rebuilt in GLSL. An analytic sphere normal, a Fresnel rim, cosine-palette thin-film interference, and a refracted lookup into the background.

    

    

      
How it works

      
  • Analytic normal. Inside the disc, z = sqrt(R² − d²) reconstructs a sphere normal with no geometry at all.
  • Fresnel rim. pow(1 − N·V, k) – the soap film is nearly invisible head-on and bright at the edge.
  • Thin-film interference approximated with a shifted cosine palette driven by film thickness, which is where the oil-slick colours come from.
  • Refraction by offsetting the background lookup along the normal, the same screen-space trick a transparent game material uses.

      
 In Bubble Factory this was a Unity Shader Graph material with premultiplied alpha and depth writes off, so overlapping bubbles layer cleanly.

    05
    

      
Parallax occlusion mapping

      Steep parallax · relief refine · self-shadow
    

  

  

    

      

        
        
live GLSL

      

      

      
A completely flat quad with real depth in it. The view ray marches down through a procedural height field until it hits, so the mortar recedes, the brick faces occlude each other, and the whole surface shifts correctly as you move the cursor.

    

    

      
How it works

      
  • March, don’t offset. Classic parallax mapping shifts the UV once and breaks at grazing angles. Here the ray steps down through 28 even height layers until the surface is above it – steep parallax – so silhouettes stay correct.
  • Relief refinement. One binary interpolation between the last two samples removes the stair-stepping that a fixed step count leaves behind. Two lines of maths, and the edges go clean.
  • Self-shadowing. From the hit point, a second short march runs back toward the light. If anything blocks it, that pixel sits in the shadow of the brick above it – the thing that actually sells the depth.
  • Normals by central difference. The height field is sampled either side of the pixel and differenced, which is exactly what a height-to-normal converter does, just evaluated live instead of baked.

      
 Parallax occlusion is a material node in both Unreal and Unity. Writing the march by hand is how I understood its real cost: every sample is another texture fetch, which is why it belongs on hero surfaces and not on a whole level.

    06
    

      
Burning dissolve

      Noise threshold · edge band · emissive rim
    

  

  

    

      

        
        
live GLSL

      

      

      
A carved panel burning away and reforming. One noise field, one moving threshold, and three smoothsteps off it produce the char, the white-hot rim and the hole – the effect every engine builds the same way.

    

    

      
How it works

      
  • One mask, three reads. A single fBm field is compared against a moving threshold three times at different offsets: past it the panel is gone, at it the rim burns, just inside it the wood chars. The whole effect is offsets on one number.
  • The gradient decides the look. Biasing the noise with a diagonal sweep makes the burn travel across the panel instead of appearing everywhere at once – the same trick as a gradient mask driving a dissolve in a material graph.
  • Emissive above 1.0. The rim colour is pushed past white so a bloom pass would catch it, which is how a dissolve reads as heat rather than as orange paint.
  • Embers gated by the mask. The particles only brighten near the burn line, so the sparks and the erosion stay locked together without any particle system.

      
 This is the dissolve I would build in Unreal as an Opacity Mask driven by a noise texture and a scalar parameter, with the edge band wired to Emissive. The Feeding uses the same threshold-on-noise idea for its fire and ember shading.
