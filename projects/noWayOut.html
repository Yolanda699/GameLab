01Overview

No Way Out is a first-person escape game set inside a real campus building at night. Class ended at 19:02 and you are still in L114. The fire alarm keeps resetting, the stairs keep folding you back onto the same floor, and a robed professor walks the corridors; if he catches you, you wake up in the classroom again. There are two ways out: find five parts and repair the lift, or find four memories hidden around the building and put the night back in order. Only the second tells you what he was really doing.

This is a two-person USC CTIN 534 project. Katrina Xiao built the whitebox, maze, gameplay systems and character AI. I handled art and technical art: props, foliage, set dressing, lighting, VFX, UI, character rigging and animation setup, and production tools.

We had three weeks to complete the building and courtyard. I used an AI-assisted Blender workflow for the prop library and worked in Maya on the hero assets, professor rig and walk-cycle revision. The workflow and division of tasks are documented in Section 09.

Blender: greybox first, then AI detailing

The level began as Katrina's whitebox: correct walls, doors, stairs and the lift, no art. My job was to turn it into the SCI building without breaking her gameplay. Same cameras, before and after:

Most props were furniture, fixtures and trim built to the dimensions in the asset list. I started with simple primitives in Blender. A Python kit (traveler_lib.py) standardised shapes, UV scale, pivots and FBX export orientation for Unity.

An AI agent connected through MCP refined the Blender greyboxes using reference photos and my review notes. It added details such as bevels, handles and cushions, and generated procedural textures for assets including the board-game atlas and leaf cards. I reviewed the renders and requested revisions before export.

  1. Brief. I describe the room or prop in real terms: its size, what it is for, what story it tells (for example, game lab 305 with a board game still half-played on the table).
  2. Generate. The agent writes or edits the round's generator script (traveler_assets_v2.py … v11.py) and runs it in Blender over MCP. Every asset is rebuilt from code, so changing a size is a rerun, not a remodel.
  3. Render for review. A preview script renders solo shots, lineups and fixed camera sweeps. Each sweep camera is named by its position and heading (m001_10.7_1.6_1.0_N0), so the same view can be compared round to round.
  4. Review and notes. I go through the renders and write the notes: parts floating off a mesh, wrong scale, a silhouette that reads as a toy. Difference images show exactly what a change touched.
  5. Lock and export. Approved assets go to Unity as SM_* FBX, and the material builder hooks them up (see art pipeline).

Look-dev in Arnold

The Arnold Standard Surface materials use the game’s texture sets, with adjustments to map packing and colour space for the Maya look-development scenes:

  1. Base colour in sRGB; metalness, roughness and normal maps in Raw.
  2. Roughness rebuilt from Unity's packed map. Unity stores smoothness in the alpha of a MetallicSmoothness texture, so I split it into separate metalness and roughness maps (roughness = 1 − smoothness).
  3. Normals run through a bump2d node set to tangent-space normals (OpenGL convention, matching the Blender export).
  4. Real-world tiling. The meshes are UV'd in metres, so each place2dTexture repeat matches the Unity tiling (fabric 0.5 m, carpet 2 m and so on), and one texture tiles correctly on every prop.

03Foliage

Foliage took three iterations. The main difficulties were flat-looking leaf cards, dark alpha edges, loss of density in the mipmaps and canopies that looked convincing at a distance but broke down from underneath.

Three rebuilds

Why round 12 wasn't enough

From a distance the cards fixed the silhouette, but up close the canopy was still a faceted solid with cards stuck on. Walking under it gave it away immediately.

The final pass, in engine

The cards still looked wrong in Unity: URP Lit made the leaves mirror-smooth, so they reflected the sky and turned blue-grey. I wrote a small custom URP shader, Traveler/Foliage, in HLSL for all leaf cards, palm fans, fronds and hedges: wrap lighting and sun translucency so shaded and back-lit leaves stay green, a sky/ground ambient term with a boost, and only part of the screen-space AO so crowns don't go muddy. Shadows, depth and depth-normals reuse URP Lit's passes, so the trees still cast and receive shadows correctly.

Under the hood

Python generators in Blender produced the trees, palms and hedges, along with procedural leaf textures. I reviewed each iteration in Blender before exporting it to Unity.

Branch and leaf-card construction

The round-13 generator grows each tree: a tapered, slightly leaning trunk with a root flare, main branches out to the crown, and twigs that reach the crown surface. Every twig tip carries a crossed pair of alpha cards, and the card texture is a sprig whose stem starts at the card's bottom edge, so every cluster visibly grows out of its twig instead of floating.

Clusters, not leaves

Each card is a whole sprig (twigs plus 6 to 13 leaves), so a few hundred cards read as thousands of leaves.

Volume normals

Card normals are rewritten to point away from the crown centre, so the whole crown shades like one soft volume and both sides of a card read the same. Bark keeps its own normals.

Variation

The magnolia mixes 85% plain clusters and 15% clusters with blooms; a few leaves show their pale underside.

Clean alpha edges

Leaf colour is dilated into the transparent area so bilinear filtering and mips don't add a dark fringe, and Unity keeps alpha coverage in the mips so crowns don't thin out with distance.

Crossed cards that grow out of each twig tipPython · traveler_assets_v10.py
def _cards(a, tip, d, size, mat, rnd, n=2, lift=.12):
    """crossed alpha cards whose bottom edge (sprig stem) sits on the twig tip, growing along d"""
    d = d.normalized(); side = d.cross(Vector((0, 0, 1)))
    if side.length < 1e-3: side = Vector((1, 0, 0))
    side.normalize(); rot0 = rnd.uniform(0, math.pi)
    for k in range(n):
        ang = rot0 + k * math.pi / n
        right = side * math.cos(ang) + d.cross(side) * math.sin(ang)
        s = size * rnd.uniform(.85, 1.15); base = tip - d * (lift * s)
        p0 = base - right * s / 2; p1 = base + right * s / 2; p2 = p1 + d * s; p3 = p0 + d * s
        a.quad_uv([tuple(p0), tuple(p1), tuple(p2), tuple(p3)], mat, [(0, 0), (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1)])

Leaf-cluster textures

The card textures are painted procedurally in Python too: a twig skeleton, leaves placed along each twig with a midrib and veins, depth-sorted so back leaves are darker, plus a height map for the normal and a gloss map. Three sets: young tree, magnolia, and magnolia in bloom.

Palms and hedges

The palms are a curved trunk, a long slim skirt of dead fronds and 22 fan fronds, each a stalk plus a fan card. Early palm tests had fronds floating free of the crown; the review renders caught it before anything reached Unity. Hedges are clipped box masses with leaf texture and the same foliage shader, so they sit in the same light as the trees.

04Set dressing

The game only works if the building feels like a place people just left. Every room got a purpose and a story taken from the real SCI building: a game lab with a half-played board game, a playtest lab with VR stations, a lecture hall, the Fish Bowl offices, a basement lab with its sofa ring and arcade cabinet, restrooms, a lounge and the courtyard. I dressed it room by room from the asset list, then ran a coverage check that logs every furniture piece and the floor it stands on, to help identify furniture floating above or intersecting the 3.6 m floors.

05Lighting

The story happens between 19:02 and 19:16, the timestamps on the first and last memory, so the lighting tells time as well as mood: a warm, still-safe dusk outside, a lantern-lit evening in the halls, cool fluorescent labs, a red alarm state that tells the player to move, and the fire grade of the finale. It all runs in real time on URP Forward+, which has no per-object light limit, so every lantern, troffer and fire is a real light.

Lighting goals

I used three goals to guide the lighting: establish the time of day, distinguish the story states and help the player navigate.

1 · Time

The night runs from 19:02 to 19:16. The sun is 6° above the horizon, so the outside stays warm and readable while the inside slowly takes over. The player should feel the minutes passing without a clock.

2 · Mood by temperature

Warm means safe, cool means institutional, red means danger. Halls are amber lanterns, labs are cool troffers, the alarm floods everything red and the finale burns orange. A screenshot's colour should tell you which part of the story you are in.

3 · Light is navigation

Lanterns hang every second bay, so corridors read as a path; the RESET panels and alarm strobes are the only saturated red; corridor ends are brighter than the middle, so the professor reads as a silhouette before you see his face.

4 · Every light has a source

Almost every light sits in a fixture I modelled (lantern, troffer, downlight, a fire), so the lighting feels like the building rather than a game. The only exception is a soft neutral hall fill that keeps corridors readable.

Light placement

I placed lights per room type, not one by one: each kind of space has a recipe (fixture model, light colour, intensity and range), and the recipe is applied to every room of that type. The plans below are top-down renders of the real level with every light marked by role; the soft discs show where each light actually reaches.

Skybox and dusk

The sky is a painted 360° panorama (4096 × 2048): lavender-grey upper sky, dappled orange and pink altocumulus, a golden band low on the horizon, distant hills and a silhouetted campus skyline with towers and palm groups. It is set on a Skybox/Panoramic material and turned so the sunset glow sits in the west, behind the low sun.

Sun

Directional, 6° above the horizon from the west, warm orange (1.0, 0.56, 0.33) at intensity 1.15, soft shadows to 35 m with 4 cascades.

Sky

Panoramic sunset, rotation 180°, exposure 0.82, tint left at the neutral default.

Ambient

Trilight instead of flat grey: sky (0.42, 0.44, 0.54), equator (0.38, 0.34, 0.35), ground (0.17, 0.15, 0.14).

Fog

Linear, 45–420 m, dusky mauve (0.56, 0.46, 0.53), so the panorama and the real buildings meet without a seam.

SSAO

Renderer feature on the art-enhanced renderer: blue-noise, depth-normals, intensity 1.1, radius 0.3, direct lighting strength 0.2, for contact shadows under furniture.

Camera

HDR, 4× MSAA, far clip 600 m so the skyline is never cut off.

Alarm state

The alarm is the one moment the building stops being warm. Sixteen red strobes in the ceiling alarms flash in alternation, but the amber lanterns stay on underneath, so the corridor still reads as a path while everything else turns hostile. The screen edge pulses red until the player reaches a RESET panel. Captured in Play mode in the art-enhanced level:

Fire grade

In the finale a post-processing volume blends in by distance to the nearest fire (full within 3 m, nothing past 14 m) and by smoke exposure: warm bloom, an ember-red vignette, contrast, white balance, slight chromatic aberration and grain. Before the finale the camera renders exactly as before.

06VFX · Fire & smoke

The finale’s fire needed to be visible across the lobby and convincing at close range. I built one reusable prefab, FX_Fire_Floor, from six particle layers plus a scorch decal and burning debris, and placed twelve of them through the ground floor with Katrina's rolling smoke and firelights.

Particle layers

One flipbook looked flat up close and disappeared across the lobby. Separate particle layers improved its depth and visibility. Here is the same fire with each layer on its own:

Soft particles

The URP depth texture is on, so flames, glow and smoke fade into the floor and walls instead of cutting hard lines.

Lit smoke

Smoke is alpha-blended Simple Lit, so the lanterns and firelight shade it instead of it glowing on its own.

HDR flames

Fire, glow and embers are additive Unlit with HDR tint so bloom catches the cores.

Gameplay-safe

The smoke column keeps the name the finale script looks for, so Katrina's smoke multiplier still drives it.

07UI & interaction

The UI had to stay quiet in a horror-adjacent game: small, dark, slightly translucent cards, with colour reserved for meaning: teal for repair and the way out, amber for memories, blue for clues and places, red for danger. Every screen is built in UI Toolkit from one C# controller and one stylesheet, scales from a 1920 × 1080 reference, and uses Inter for interface text and Lora Italic for thoughts and memories. All screenshots below are from the current build of the art-enhanced level.

Interaction outline

Picking things up in a cluttered room needs a clear "this one". I wrote a two-pass screen-space outline: a mask pass draws the targeted object, an outline pass dilates it. Because it reads depth, walls and partial cover clip the outline correctly, and it only appears within seven metres, to keep the highlight limited to nearby, visible objects.

Re-rigging in Maya for variation

The imported FBX had a baked animation and needed rescaling for the building. I built a control rig in Maya for the poses and motion we needed, saving a separate scene at each stage. The main changes were:

Real height

Scaled to about 1.83 m so he sits at the right size in the 1 unit = 1 m building.

Unity-safe joints

Joint names kept identical to the source FBX, so anything baked off the bind joints still drives the model already in Unity.

Our states

Poses for what he actually does in the game: patrol, alert look-around, accusing point, crouching search, chase.

The robe

He is mostly robe, so the robe and skirt got their own secondary controls for crouches and long strides.

Iterating the walk: Maya, then back into Unity

The original walk has the professor’s hands clasped under his chin. For the patrol, I wanted relaxed arms and more movement through the torso. I revised the cycle on the Maya rig and reimported it into Unity, retaining the source leg timing as described below.

Legs kept

The leg and hip timing come from the pack cycle: I sampled the footfalls in Unity and placed the IK feet and COG on them in Maya, so the stride still matches his NavMesh speed and the robe still clears his knees.

Arms that swing

Hands unclasped with a relaxed curl. Each arm swings against its leg with a two-frame lag, and the elbow bends more on the forward swing and trails the shoulder by a few frames.

Torso and head

Shoulders counter-rotate against the hips, the spine leans forward and gives a little on each footfall, and the head turns against the shoulders so his eyes stay on the corridor.

Back into Unity

Same joint names and bind pose on both sides (checked: 0.00° difference on all 44 joints), so each frame converts straight across Maya's right-handed axes, layers over the pack's legs and robe, and bakes into a new clip.

Only where it's safe

The art-enhanced level runs a copy of the animator controller with the new walk; the other levels keep the original.

If I took it further: the alert look-around and crouching search poses from the v05 lineup would become new states for the professor, through the same Maya-to-Unity route.

Two editor tools in Tools › Traveler make import one click: texture import rules (1024 max, 8× anisotropic, linear metallic-smoothness, alpha coverage kept in mips for foliage) and Build URP Materials, which reads one TravelerMaterials.json and builds every material with tiling set from real-world size.

    if (nrm) { mat.SetTexture("_BumpMap", nrm); mat.EnableKeyword("_NORMALMAP"); }
    if (ms)  { mat.SetTexture("_MetallicGlossMap", ms); mat.EnableKeyword("_METALLICSPECGLOSSMAP"); }

    // meshes are UV'd in metres (1 UV = 1 m), so tiling = 1 / texture size in metres
    float s = 1f / Mathf.Max(0.01f, d.tileMeters);
    mat.SetTextureScale("_BaseMap", new Vector2(s, s));

    if (d.alphaClip > 0) {                         // leaves, fronds, decals
        mat.SetFloat("_AlphaClip", 1); mat.SetFloat("_Cutoff", d.alphaClip);
        mat.renderQueue = (int)RenderQueue.AlphaTest;
    }
    mat.SetFloat("_Cull", d.doubleSided ? 0f : 2f);
}

Challenge

Hundreds of lights in a small team's budget

A lit building at night needs a light in every lantern and fixture, far beyond forward rendering's per-object limit.

Solution

URP Forward+ and light roles

Forward+ removes the per-object cap; lights are grouped by role (lantern, room, fire, alarm) so each mood is a handful of decisions, not 326.

Challenge

Fire visibility

A single flipbook looked flat up close and disappeared across the lobby.

Solution

Layered particle effects

Flames for shape, base for contact, glows for heat on the floor, embers for scale, lit smoke for depth, plus real firelight.

Challenge

Close-up foliage

Blob canopies, then cards on a blob, both fell apart once the player walked under them.

Solution

Branch structure and canopy shading

Branches out to the crown surface, sprig cards growing from every twig tip, normals pointing out from the crown centre.

Challenge

125 props, one look

Individually configured materials can differ in tiling, filtering and shader settings.

Solution

Metre-scale UVs and a material manifest

Every mesh UV'd in metres, every material generated from one JSON file, every texture configured on import.
